    brushy still life (inkwell, coffee cup, fountain pen) backs the
    step-by-step section, and a farmhouse landscape fills the footer. A
    small watercolor bee sits inside faint geometric construction lines,

    product UI appears as a screen in a frosted, glassmorphism browser
    window over the sky, and as layered cards floating on the still life.
    Where the paintings meet the cream, they break up into dithering: a
    grid of tiny dots instead of a smooth fade.
  why: >-
    The designer explains the overall idea: the founder wanted the site to
    feel "homey," not like enterprise software, because the buyers here
    are "mom-and-pop title companies". The base illustrations were made
    with Midjourney, as "watercolor countryside, cottage" scenes. For the
    edges, "a standard blur/gradient looked flat next to the painterly art
    style", so the designer built a small tool that applies "ordered Bayer
    dithering to fade the illustration into the background color
    pixel-by-pixel instead of blending — it reads as grain, not gradient,
    and matches the hand-painted texture instead of fighting it."

    In detail: (Our read: the countryside answers what a title company
    is really for, a family getting the keys to a home, so the paperwork
    software is shown inside that picture rather than in a cold dashboard.
    The stamp frame and the bee add small handmade touches, and keeping
    the real product UI crisp on top of the paintings says the warmth is
    the setting, not a replacement for the tool.)

  what: >-
    On load, an intro animation builds the hero: the parentheses appear
    first, then the branches, painted sky and product screen, and finally
    the headline arrives as a staggered blur-in, word by word. After that
    the hero never fully stops: the painted branches move gently in an
    idle animation, made with turbulence displacement (an SVG "wind"
    filter whose animated noise ripples the leaves). The "Hours of work"
    section is a set of auto-advancing tabs: every few seconds the next
    numbered step opens, a thin line fills under the active step, and the
    card on the painting changes, its rows stacking in and an address
    typing itself in. The three feature cards play small Lottie
    animations on a loop: a task list ticking off, a chat answer
    typewriting itself, an iMessage thread. Big section headings use a
    scroll-linked text fill, darkening letter by letter as you scroll and
    stopping when you stop, and the "Hours of work" section waits for a
    scroll-reveal, only playing once it comes into view. Buttons darken slightly on hover.
  why: >-
    No statement from the designers about motion was found, so this is
    our interpretation. The wind in the branches is the clever part: it
    makes a still painting feel like a real summer afternoon without the
    weight of a video, and it keeps the "homey" mood alive while you read.
    The self-playing tabs and Lottie cards let a complicated back-office
    process demonstrate itself in small, calm steps, so a small title
    company can see the work being done without clicking through a demo.
    The scroll-linked text fill slows you down on the key promises
    ("Automated work, human safety"), matching the unhurried pace of the
    rest of the page.

screens set inside the scenery to feel "homey" rather than enterprise.
